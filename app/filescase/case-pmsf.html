01 — The Problem

A prestigious institution,
held back by its own website.

The Princess Maha Chakri Sirindhorn Foundation is a royal-patronage organisation with a significant public profile in Thailand. Its existing website, however, failed to reflect that standing — outdated aesthetics, confusing navigation, and a layout that broke completely on mobile devices.

As a freelance UX/UI designer working within a cross-functional team alongside developers, my task was to redesign the entire public-facing website: modernise the visual identity, restructure the information architecture, and ensure the result worked flawlessly across every screen size.

How Might We
"How might we bring the Foundation's website up to the standard of its reputation — while making it simple enough for any visitor to find what they need in under two clicks?"

Navigation Restructure

The original site had 11 top-level navigation items with no clear priority order. The redesign reduced this to 5 primary items with logical sub-groups.

Research Insights

Insight 01 — Audience is diverse
Visitors range from media and government officials to school students and general public. The redesign needed to serve both formal and casual reading contexts simultaneously.
Insight 02 — Programmes are the core
80% of meaningful visits were programme-related. Yet programmes were buried 3–4 levels deep. Making them front-and-centre was the single highest-impact structural change.
Insight 03 — Trust signals matter
The Foundation's royal patronage is its core credibility. The redesign needed to communicate gravitas and official standing immediately — without feeling cold or inaccessible.

04 — Design

A visual identity worthy
of royal patronage.

The design direction drew from Thai institutional visual language — dignified, structured, and warm — using a deep maroon primary colour referencing the Foundation's official palette, paired with white space and clear typographic hierarchy.

Key Design Decisions

Colour System
Deep maroon (#8B1A3A) as the primary brand colour — drawn from the Foundation's official palette — paired with clean white space. Warm secondary tones for programme categories (green, amber, blue) provide differentiation without fragmentation.
Typography
A single type family with clear size hierarchy: display heading at 40px+ for authority, body at 16px for readability, labels in uppercase monospace for categorisation. Thai and English text handled with appropriate line-height for both scripts.
Programme Card Pattern
Reusable card component for all programmes — colour-coded by category, always showing title, brief description, and a clear "read more" action. Used consistently across homepage, listing, and detail pages.
Responsive Grid
CSS Grid-based layout with three breakpoints: 4-column desktop (1200px+), 2-column tablet (768–1199px), single-column mobile (under 768px). All images fluid-resized. Navigation collapses to a hamburger below 768px.
05 — Test & Iterate

What the team surfaced
in review rounds.

Working within a cross-functional team with developers and foundation stakeholders, I participated in three review rounds before final delivery.

Before

The hero banner used a large static image with text overlaid directly. On smaller screens the text became unreadable against the image.

→
After

Switched to a solid brand-colour hero with a subtle radial gradient. Text is always legible regardless of screen size, and load time improved significantly by removing the large hero image.

Before

Programme detail pages had no clear call-to-action. Stakeholders noted visitors were reading about programmes but had no obvious next step (enquire, apply, donate).

→
After

Added a persistent sticky CTA panel on all programme detail pages with contextually appropriate actions. "Learn more", "Get involved", and "Contact us" buttons tailored per programme type.

Before

Thai-language body text was using the same line-height as English. The result was lines colliding with each other because Thai characters have taller ascenders.

→
After

Increased line-height for Thai body text from 1.5 to 1.8 across all pages. A small but critical change that dramatically improved legibility for the primary-language audience.

What I learned: Working within a cross-functional team for a high-profile institutional client taught me how to advocate for UX decisions in a context where brand constraints and organisational sensitivities carry significant weight. Every design choice needed clear rationale — "it looks better" was never enough.

What I'd do differently: I would have pushed harder for user testing with actual visitors to the Foundation's site early in the process. The stakeholder team had strong opinions, but those opinions weren't always aligned with what public visitors actually needed. Even one round of guerrilla testing on a prototype would have surfaced that faster.

What this project gave me: My first professional freelance UX engagement. It showed me I could manage a project end-to-end — research, design, iteration, stakeholder presentations, developer handoff — independently and on a real deadline.
